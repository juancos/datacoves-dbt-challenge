$740 or $610? Why Your Business Needs a Number It Can Trust

A small payment example makes the business case for dbt: clear definitions, repeatable calculations, and checks you can inspect.

Juan José Pardo Data Quality & Analytics

Imagine a Monday leadership meeting. Someone asks a simple question: how much money did we collect? Two reports are open on the table. One says $740. The other says $610.

Nobody is lying and nobody made an arithmetic error. The two reports answer different questions. One totals everything customers were charged. The other subtracts what was refunded and counts only the orders that were actually completed. Both are reasonable definitions of "money collected." Only one of them answers what the room is asking.

What follows is twenty minutes spent reconciling two numbers instead of making a decision. That meeting is the problem worth solving, not the arithmetic.

Six orders, small enough to check by hand

Here is a deliberately tiny example: four customers, six orders, eight payment transactions. It is fictional, and small enough that you can check every figure yourself.

Two orders are worth a closer look. Order 103 was cancelled: the customer was charged $80 and refunded the whole $80, so it contributed nothing. Order 104 was completed with a partial refund: $200 paid, $50 refunded, $150 collected.

That is also why the two ways of counting land in the same place here. Subtract every refund from every charge and you get $610. Add up the net amounts on the five completed orders and you get $610 again, because the cancelled order nets to zero. In a real business, where cancellations are not always refunded in full, those two methods drift apart. Which is why the definition needs to live somewhere other than in someone's spreadsheet.

What dbt actually contributes

The fix is not a bigger dashboard. It is agreeing on one definition and making it repeatable.

That is what dbt is for. It is a tool used by data teams to transform data that already sits in your database. The team writes the calculation once, deciding what counts as a completed order and how refunds are handled, and dbt runs it the same way every time, with checks attached and documentation produced alongside it.

In business terms, that buys you four things:

  • Shared rules. "Net collections" is defined in one place, so Finance and Marketing stop arriving with different totals.
  • Automatic checks. A missing order ID, a status nobody recognizes, a payment pointing at an order that does not exist: these show up when the data is built, not in the meeting.
  • Reviewable changes. The calculation lives in version-controlled code, so changing the definition is proposed, reviewed and dated like any other decision.
  • Traceable results. Anyone can look up how a number was produced.

It is worth being equally clear about what dbt does not do. It does not decide the business definition; that is a judgment call, and Finance should own it. It does not take over Finance's responsibility for the number. And it does not repair incorrect source data. It tells you the data is wrong, early and loudly, but someone still has to go fix it.

What I built to test this

To keep the argument concrete, I built the example above as a working project.

dbt is installed and runs locally against MotherDuck, a cloud database. Three CSV files are loaded using dbt seeds: customers, orders and payments. One table model calculates net collections for each completed order. Payments are aggregated by order before they are joined, so an order with three payment rows still appears once instead of three times. That single detail is where a lot of hand-built revenue reports quietly go wrong.

A full build runs 3 seeds, 1 model and 23 tests: 27 successful steps.

The 23 tests check the structure of the data: unique IDs, required values, accepted status values, and valid relationships between tables. They do not reconcile the financial total.

The total itself I verified separately, by querying MotherDuck directly: five completed orders, $610.00 in net collections. The tests tell you the data is in good shape. The query tells you the number matches the definition. Those are two different promises, and it is worth keeping them apart.

What this demonstration does not prove

  • It is a small demonstration, not a production financial system.
  • Net collections here are not recognized accounting revenue.
  • Results are attributed to order date, not payment date.
  • Production use would need reconciliation against source systems, explicit rules for currency and refund timing, and ongoing monitoring.

What I'm asking for

A four-week pilot, on one metric we already argue about.

Finance owns the definition. The data team implements and maintains it. We run it alongside the existing report for the full four weeks rather than switching a critical report over.

That gives us enough to judge three things: how much reconciliation effort the current process consumes, how many discrepancies show up between the two versions, and how long it takes to change the calculation when someone asks for a variation.

The costs are real and worth naming up front: engineering time to build it, training time for the analysts, database usage, and whatever we choose for hosting and scheduling. I have not attached a return figure to this, because I do not have one yet. That is what the pilot is for.

The request is narrow on purpose: one metric, four weeks, run in parallel, measured against what we do today. If the discrepancies do not shrink and the definition is no easier to change, we stop, and we have learned that cheaply. If they do, we will be deciding how far to take this from evidence rather than from a recommendation.
